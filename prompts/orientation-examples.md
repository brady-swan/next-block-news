# Next Block News — orientation examples

This is the human-curated staging ledger for examples that may later be incorporated into
the live newsroom orientation. Adding an entry here does **not** change the production
prompt. Review and promote examples deliberately so one-off reactions do not silently
become system policy.

Each entry should preserve the useful editorial core, identify the failure mode or drift,
and state the reusable lesson. Prefer real NBN work over invented examples.

## 2026-09-02 — Bitcoin/gold correlation: current condition, not trading signal

**Source candidate:** The Block, “Bitcoin and gold move in lockstep as debasement trade
gains more steam.” The original page could not be fetched directly; the desk inspected a
secondary page repeating the reported figures. Typefully draft `10593702` was subsequently
edited and published by Brady.

**Approved core framing:**

> Bitcoin’s correlation with gold has reached a record high as both assets benefit from
> the debasement trade.

**What made the story useful:** The record correlation is an observable Bitcoin market
condition. Connecting it to simultaneous demand for scarce assets explains why the data
matters to a Bitcoin reader without bolting on generic macro commentary.

**What the generated draft got wrong:** It moved from reporting the current relationship
into selected historical analogues about 172% and 350% Bitcoin rallies, then added a Fear &
Greed reading. That changed the genre from news and explanation into technical analysis and
an implied trading signal.

**Reusable lesson:** Use market data to explain present conditions and the monetary context.
Do not turn correlations, chart patterns, sentiment gauges, or prior cycles into forecasts,
price targets, rally analogues, or trading setups. Historical context belongs only when it
clarifies the reported development rather than suggesting what the reader should expect or
do next.

## 2026-09-02 — Capital B financing: below the treasury-company bar

**Published post:** [Capital B private placement](https://x.com/nextblocknews_/status/2095134649886691823),
linked to a generic Phemex news page.

**What the generated post did:** It devoted a long paragraph to a €7.6 million private
placement, share premium, four warrant tranches, potential additional financing, and a
possible 376 BTC purchase by Capital B.

**Owner judgment:** Do not post this. Capital B is outside the focused treasury-company set
and this financing does not clear the high bar for covering another treasury company. Adam
Back's participation and detailed deal mechanics do not make a routine potential Bitcoin buy
important to the reader.

**Reusable lesson:** Outside Strategy, Metaplanet, and Strive, treasury-company activity needs
an exceptional reason to exist on the feed: genuine market consequence, a major household
name, a category-changing first, or another development that materially changes the reader's
picture. Financing terms and a possible future purchase by a small issuer are not enough.
Apply the importance test before researching or polishing the transaction details.

## 2026-09-02 — Ireland savings wrapper: right story, overwritten copy

**Published post:** [Ireland's tax-advantaged savings account](https://x.com/nextblocknews_/status/2095137919740887401),
linked to Brefcrypto.

**What made the story useful:** Ireland designed a new tax-advantaged investment account that
can hold listed securities and funds while excluding direct Bitcoin ownership. That is a
concrete state-policy decision affecting Bitcoin's treatment, not merely another official
expressing skepticism about “crypto.” This is a definite post.

**What the generated post got wrong:** It overexplained the European Commission background,
UCITS eligibility, the distinction from a purchase ban, the unconfirmed treatment of a
Bitcoin-exposed ETF, and future budget details. Some context was useful, but the cumulative
detail buried a simple policy development. The long second paragraph also needed more blank
line breaks for an X reader.

**Reusable lesson:** Once the key policy distinction is clear, stop. Keep only the context
that changes the reader's understanding. Longer posts should still use short, scannable
paragraphs—usually one or two sentences—with blank lines between them. Story weight may
justify length; it does not justify a wall of text.

The Capital B and Ireland posts mark a healthy next phase: the desk is producing enough
plausible work that selection and editing quality, rather than a total lack of candidates,
is now the problem to solve.

## 2026-09-02 — Strategy cost-of-capital explanation: good story, transaction-audit excess

**Typefully draft:** `10594842`

**Story and receipt:** Strategy CEO Phong Le's explanation of the company's roughly 7,000 BTC
sale near $60,000-$65,000 and later 4,603 BTC purchase at an $80,318 average, linked to
[Crypto.news](https://crypto.news/strategy-ceo-explains-60k-bitcoin-sales-80k-buys/).

**Submitted copy (verbatim):**

> Strategy CEO Phong Le went on Bloomberg Crypto to defend the company's recent bitcoin
> trading, arguing the firm doesn't buy or sell based on bitcoin's price but on its own cost
> of capital.
>
> The numbers: Strategy sold roughly 7,000 BTC in a series of disclosed reductions this
> summer (2,225 BTC in early July, 1,638 BTC in early August, 1,690 BTC the following week,
> plus an earlier ~$218.4M sale) to fund preferred-dividend obligations, at prices in the
> $60,000-$65,000 range. It then bought 4,603 BTC for $369.7 million — an average of $80,318
> — between Aug. 24-30, funded partly by a $602.8 million common-share sale at a premium to
> book value.
>
> Le called the sell "the right trade at the time" given the company's financing conditions
> then versus now. Strategy also says its USD assets ($6.71B) now nearly offset its
> convertible debt ($6.75B), producing a company-defined net leverage of 0.0% — a metric that
> doesn't erase its preferred-stock obligations or convertible notes, just nets dollar
> holdings against debt principal. Total holdings stand at 845,050 BTC acquired for $63.73B,
> averaging $75,412/BTC.

**Assessment:** Publish after a substantial trim. This clears the Strategy bar because it is
not merely another purchase announcement: the CEO is explaining why selling Bitcoin near
$60,000 and buying again near $80,000 can both make sense under Strategy's capital-allocation
framework. That is fresh, counterintuitive, and useful. It also advances beyond a block item
that merely reported the latest purchase.

**What is good:** The first paragraph finds the real story immediately: cost of capital, not
the spot price, governed the decisions. The submitted copy also avoids presenting the trades
as market timing and appropriately qualifies Strategy's self-defined zero-leverage metric.

**What should change:** The middle becomes a transaction audit, enumerating individual sale
tranches that do not help the reader understand the decision. The final paragraph then opens
a second balance-sheet story—cash, convertibles, preferred obligations, total holdings, and
average basis. Those facts are defensible but bury the clean explanatory insight and repeat
Strategy figures already circulating on the feed. Keep the sale total, later purchase, source
of funding, and Le's rationale; let the rest go. Two short paragraphs are enough.

**How I would write it:**

> Strategy CEO Phong Le says the company's sale of roughly 7,000 BTC near $60,000-$65,000
> and its later purchase of 4,603 BTC at an $80,318 average were both driven by Strategy's
> cost of capital—not a view on Bitcoin's price.
>
> Le said Strategy sold bitcoin when it needed to fund preferred dividends and issuing stock
> was unattractive. When MSTR later traded at a premium, the company raised $602.8 million by
> selling shares and used part of the proceeds to resume buying bitcoin. He called the earlier
> sale “the right trade at the time.”

**Reusable lesson:** A familiar company can still produce a new story when its decision-maker
explains a counterintuitive action. Lead with the explanation. Do not reproduce every
transaction and balance-sheet figure simply because the research desk verified it.

## 2026-09-02 — Sality disruption: strong Bitcoin-security story, lead with the payload

**Typefully draft:** `10594844`

**Story and receipt:** CrowdStrike and international law enforcement disrupted the Sality
peer-to-peer botnet, whose primary payload for eight years replaced copied cryptocurrency
addresses, linked to [CrowdStrike's primary report](https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/blog/inside-sality-botnet-disruption-operation/).

**Submitted copy (verbatim):**

> CrowdStrike and international law enforcement dismantled the Sality botnet on Aug. 31 — a
> piece of malware that had targeted crypto users' clipboards for years as part of a P2P
> criminal network running since 2003.
>
> The operation, done in partnership with the DOJ, FBI, DoD's Defense Criminal Investigative
> Service, Shadowserver Foundation, Europol, Eurojust and law enforcement in Bulgaria,
> Hungary and Romania, sinkholed the botnet's peer-to-peer command channel across more than
> 15,000 infected machines worldwide. One of Sality's payloads watched clipboards for copied
> bitcoin addresses and silently swapped in attacker-controlled addresses before a payment
> went through — a classic address-clipping attack that only works if a victim doesn't verify
> the destination address before sending.
>
> The case is a reminder that malware targeting self-custody doesn't need to break bitcoin's
> cryptography — it just needs to intercept the address a user copies and pastes.

**Assessment:** Publish after a moderate edit. This is a concrete Bitcoin-security story,
not generic cybercrime: the malware directly targeted copied Bitcoin addresses, the botnet
reached more than 15,000 machines, and a primary source documents a coordinated disruption.
It remains useful without a `NEW:` label because the operation occurred two days ago and the
technical lesson is not perishable.

**What is good:** The copy makes the Bitcoin relevance explicit and ends on the right
distinction: endpoint security can defeat a self-custody user without defeating Bitcoin's
cryptography. It uses a strong primary receipt and explains address clipping in plain
language.

**What should change:** “Dismantled” is stronger than CrowdStrike's own “disrupted”; the
primary report says existing malware remains active even though the operator's command
channel is inert. The long roster of agencies consumes the most prominent paragraph without
adding much reader value. The lead should foreground the eight-year address-replacement
payload, which is the reason this belongs on a Bitcoin feed. The source says the clipper
replaced both Bitcoin and Ethereum addresses; mentioning that does not dilute the Bitcoin
story and is more faithful than silently narrowing the claim.

**How I would write it:**

> CrowdStrike and international law enforcement have disrupted Sality, a peer-to-peer botnet
> whose main payload spent eight years replacing copied Bitcoin and Ethereum addresses with
> addresses controlled by its operator.
>
> Sality reached more than 15,000 machines and stole at least $150,000 in cryptocurrency,
> according to CrowdStrike. The Aug. 31 operation cut the operator off from the network,
> although malware already installed on infected devices still must be removed.
>
> The attack never had to break Bitcoin's cryptography. It intercepted the destination
> address before the transaction was signed.

**Reusable lesson:** For a broad security event, lead with the exact mechanism that makes it
a Bitcoin story. Prefer the primary source's precise operational claim over a stronger newsy
verb, and compress institutional rosters unless a particular participant changes the story.

## 2026-09-02 — Kimi/Bitcoin Red Team sweep: strong idea, unresolved provenance

**Typefully draft:** `10597509`

**Story and receipts:** A Kimi K3-assisted Bitcoin Red Team sweep reportedly flagged 7,958
potential findings across roughly 390 projects; the submitted post connects that campaign to
an exploited BTCPay Server/LND credential flaw. It links readers to [BTCPay Server's August 7
security advisory](https://blog.btcpayserver.org/security-advisory-btcpay-server-2-4-2/) and
also relies on an inspected edgeX article for the AI-sweep figures and connection.

**Submitted copy (verbatim):**

> An AI-assisted security sweep of Bitcoin's open-source stack has already produced a real,
> exploited vulnerability. Over a two-week campaign, Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 model — used by
> the volunteer "Bitcoin Red Team" — cataloged 7,958 potential findings across roughly 390
> projects, 1,280 rated high or critical severity.
>
> One of those findings was real and was actively exploited: BTCPay Server disclosed a
> critical flaw, present in every version before 2.4.2, that let an unauthenticated remote
> attacker pull .macaroon credential files from LND nodes — enough to take control of a
> Lightning node and move its funds. BTCPay confirmed attackers exploited it and that funds
> were stolen before the fix shipped. Only LND deployments are affected; other Lightning
> implementations and BTCPay's own on-chain wallets are not. The project has patched to 2.4.2
> (with LND 0.21.1) and is urging any LND user on an older version to update immediately or
> take their server offline.
>
> Context on the scale: at time of reporting only about a quarter of the 7,958 AI-flagged
> findings had been independently reproduced and under 30% had reached upstream maintainers,
> so the headline count isn't a confirmed-exploit tally — but the sweep targeted wallets and
> Lightning infrastructure specifically, not Bitcoin's base consensus layer, which hasn't
> been shown to be compromised.

**Assessment:** Hold for source repair and a substantial trim; do not publish this draft as
submitted. The underlying story is highly relevant and potentially excellent: AI-assisted
review of Bitcoin infrastructure apparently surfaced actionable security work. But the linked
BTCPay advisory is nearly a month old and supports only the vulnerability, exploitation,
scope, and mitigation facts. It does not say Kimi K3 found the flaw or support the 7,958,
390-project, 1,280-severity, reproduction, or disclosure figures. Those claims come from a
low-authority edgeX page that reads like syndicated material and even appends unrelated market
feed content. The current evidence does not securely establish the post's central causal link.

**What is good:** This is exactly the kind of technical Bitcoin story the feed should seek.
The copy correctly distinguishes surrounding wallet/Lightning infrastructure from Bitcoin's
consensus layer, accurately narrows the BTCPay impact to LND deployments, and cautions that
7,958 automated findings are not 7,958 confirmed vulnerabilities. The BTCPay primary source
does confirm exploitation, stolen funds, affected versions, and urgent update guidance.

**What should change:** Find an original Bitcoin Red Team, researcher, project, interview, or
other credible receipt that directly connects the Kimi campaign to the BTCPay disclosure and
owns the sweep figures. Until then, do not lead with “has already produced” as an established
fact. Once sourced, cut the post roughly in half: the scan's scale and verification caveat are
the new story; the month-old BTCPay incident is the concrete example, not a second full
security advisory. Avoid stacking every affected/unaffected implementation detail.

**How I would write it, once the campaign provenance is secured:**

> An AI-assisted review of nearly 390 Bitcoin open-source projects flagged 7,958 potential
> security issues in two weeks, including 1,280 initially rated high or critical, according
> to the Bitcoin Red Team.
>
> Those are leads, not confirmed vulnerabilities: only about a quarter had been reproduced
> at the time of reporting. But the campaign says one disclosure led to BTCPay Server's 2.4.2
> fix for a flaw that exposed LND macaroon credentials. BTCPay separately confirmed attackers
> had exploited that vulnerability and stolen funds.
>
> The incident affected Lightning-node infrastructure, not Bitcoin's consensus rules or
> BTCPay's on-chain wallets.

**Reusable lesson:** A strong primary receipt for the example does not automatically prove the
larger news peg or causal connection. When the story is “this research campaign produced this
real-world result,” secure a source that owns that linkage. Then lead with the fresh campaign,
use the older incident as concise evidence of consequence, and keep automated-finding counts
clearly separated from verified vulnerabilities.
